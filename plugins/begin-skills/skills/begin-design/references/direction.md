# Direction and composition

## Make the subject visible

Write a brief with four useful decisions: the visitor's situation, the page's main message, the action, and the atmosphere. Use the subject's work, materials, language, or constraints to find a distinctive element. A consultant can show an annotated design decision; a photographer can let a sequence of images lead; a product can demonstrate the actual task it helps with.

Choose a direction from the brief, not from an automatic visual recipe. Editorial, expressive, restrained, dense, and playful compositions can all work. Match their emphasis to the content and the visitor's attention. Preserve a requested look rather than treating a familiar style as a mistake.

Choose the content's spatial relationship with [composition choices](composition.md): a broad artifact under a statement, work that leads, an image scene, a typographic field, a collection, an opening action, a shared stage, or useful adjacent regions. For a new substantial direction, compare two or three arrangements using the same material before retaining the starter's structure. Judge their whole-page continuation and phone order as well as their opening.

## Turn direction into a small system

Choose type from the actual wording and intended character. Inspect proportions, stroke contrast, characteristic letters and the shape of a complete headline. A family that reads comfortably in a guide may give a poster too little expression. Decide display, body and utility roles separately; one family can still serve several roles.

Tune weight, width, spacing and line height together. Use the family's available weights and, where supplied, width or optical-size axes. Confirm that the loaded font supports those choices. Read several body paragraphs and the important labels at their actual size. Tune measure and line height to the language and typeface rather than squeezing text to fit a screenshot. Keep language coverage, loading and licensing suitable for the delivered site.

Name color roles by purpose: background, surface, text, muted text, action, focus, and status. Check important text and controls against their actual backgrounds. Decorative color must not be the only way to distinguish a state.

Use a spacing rhythm that explains relationships. Keep a label close to its content and related actions together. Let major sections breathe more than items within them. Grids should support reading order; a large screen's visual placement must not produce a confusing DOM order on a phone.

## Give each section a job

The introduction establishes the offer or subject. Work examples reveal something specific. Services help visitors recognize a fit. A process sets expectations. Contact lets them act. Remove repeated statements that compete for the same job.

Let those jobs determine each section's proportions and arrangement. Follow a large opening with the next useful evidence or decision; a narrower reading passage, broad work view, ruled sequence, or compact contact area may each serve a different moment. Keep alignment and type roles coherent while giving important material the space it needs.

Choose images for what they communicate. Include attribution or usage information where required. Use original diagrams or labeled concept work when client images are unavailable. Do not invent client logos, project outcomes, or personal credentials to fill empty space.

## Critique the result

Compare the rendered site with the brief. Ask whether the strongest element communicates the subject, whether the eye follows the intended order, and whether the main action is clear. Change the part that causes the problem. A different accent color alone does not fix generic content or weak hierarchy.
