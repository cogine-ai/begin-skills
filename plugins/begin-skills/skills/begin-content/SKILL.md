# Begin content

Help the reader understand, decide, or learn something useful. Use the owner's actual offer, experience, vocabulary, and material. Write natural English for the intended audience; keep their brand voice. Clarity should make that voice easier to understand, including when the audience spans North America and Europe.

Start with the reader's question, the intended result, and the available material. Inspect accepted content before asking for it again. Ask only for missing facts that change the current output. Where facts are unavailable, use a neutral description, mark an unresolved claim in the draft, or label illustrative material; do not invent numbers, endorsements, customers, or experience.

Choose the relevant method and read only what the task needs:

- [Website writing](references/writing.md): page stories, service and product explanations, marketing copy, and interface text.
- [Content planning](references/content-planning.md): turn notes, existing pages, transcripts, and incomplete material into a useful content plan.
- [Researched articles](references/researched-articles.md): answer a question through verified sources, original reasoning, drafting, revision, and publication preparation.
- [Complete service-site article](references/examples/first-service-website-blog.md): an original brief, source/claim record, full article, and editorial review. Read it when a complete worked example helps.

Give each section a clear job. A page can explain an offer and invite an appropriate action. An article can resolve a question, teach, compare, or develop a supported view; it need not end in a sales call to action. Use familiar terms on controls and keep them consistent through the flow.

Keep sourced facts, the owner's supplied experience, interpretation, and recommendations distinguishable. Open material sources before relying on them. Put citations near the claims they support and preserve important limits. If source access is unavailable, explain the affected claim and continue with the verified or supplied material.

Draft with the actual publication format in mind. Usually omit a terminal full stop from display titles; keep meaningful question marks and internal sentence boundaries. Add a subtitle or eyebrow only when it supplies needed information. Check headline wrapping, reading order, useful headings, link labels, and mobile reading when a preview is available. For interface content, check hints, errors, and actual completion messages. For technical products, explain the reader's task before internal mechanisms and tie capability claims to the implementation.

Edit for meaning and support before polishing tone. Remove repeated points, empty adjectives, and phrases that could describe any business. Preserve content and claims the user has explicitly approved unless the requested edit affects them. Finish with the requested copy or plan and the few unresolved facts or publication requirements that matter. A draft, a rendered page, and a published article are different outcomes.
