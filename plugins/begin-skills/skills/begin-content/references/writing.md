# Writing a website people can use

## Start with the reader's questions

An introduction answers what this is, whom it helps or interests, and what the visitor can do next. A useful headline names an outcome, subject, or offer. A short explanation adds information rather than repeating the headline. Name the action plainly: “Explore the work”, “Book a call”, or “Send an enquiry” when that is what actually happens.

Service content explains a recognizable job and the collaboration involved. Product content explains the task, shows a concrete use, and identifies the next step. Portfolio content gives the work context: the question, an important decision, and what the work illustrates. The page's sections need not follow a fixed sales formula.

## Make stories specific

Connect background, present motivation, and the visitor's benefit. Use a real detail supplied by the owner: how their work began, how they approach a problem, or why they made the product. Keep the person visible without creating fictional client anecdotes.

For concept projects, say they are concepts or self-initiated work. Describe choices and exploration without claiming customer adoption or commercial results. Keep placeholders recognizable during editing and replace them before representing the site as the owner's finished content.

## Write English naturally

Prefer familiar verbs, concrete nouns, and active sentences. Use enough context to make the meaning clear. Keep one thought per section, vary sentence length naturally, and preserve appropriate specialist terms for the audience. Avoid generic promises such as “revolutionize your journey” and repeated contrast slogans. A brand can be expressive without making every sentence an advertisement.

Name the actor when it matters: “Your team can review enquiries” is clearer than “Enquiries can be reviewed.” Use the same term for the same thing across headlines, controls, and confirmations. Explain an unfamiliar term at its first useful appearance. Keep essential conditions close to the promise: “Export the report after connecting your account” communicates a different capability from “Export any report.”

Match punctuation and capitalization to the owner's voice. Review headlines in the rendered layout; shorten or rephrase a broken headline rather than adding arbitrary nonbreaking spaces everywhere.

### Editorial checks for Agent output

Owner review of the local Agent examples identified two habits worth checking explicitly. Treat these as conditional editing prompts, not a claim about every model output or a required style for every brand.

- **Heading punctuation:** usually omit a final full stop from page titles, section titles and short labels. A heading's position and type already separate it from the following text. Keep a question mark, a meaningful internal sentence boundary, or deliberate brand punctuation when it serves the wording. Read the title as a title rather than automatically punctuating it as body prose.
- **Supporting lines:** do not automatically add a subtitle, eyebrow or introductory paragraph to every heading. Start with the heading and actual content. Add a supporting line only when it contributes information the reader needs: the subject, audience, scope, condition or next action. Delete it when it repeats the title, praises the layout or merely fills a familiar headline-plus-subtitle pattern. Preserve useful instructions and distinct body content.

For example, use “Your plan guide” without “A useful little guide”. “For curious adults. No experience needed.” adds an audience and entry condition, so it can remain beside a workshop invitation. Judge the actual page after removal; the aim is useful content and clear hierarchy rather than uniformly sparse pages.

For North American and European audiences, use the owner's chosen English spelling consistently. Preserve recognizable product names and local terms. Spell out ambiguous dates, and state a currency or time zone when the next action depends on it. These choices support comprehension; they need not erase a distinctive voice or constrain copy to a controlled vocabulary.

## Explain technical products to visitors

Start with a task the visitor recognizes, then explain the capability that supports it. “Keep client messages in one inbox” can introduce the benefit; the next paragraph can explain who has access and which messages are included. Mention hosting, models, APIs, or databases when they affect the visitor's choice, setup, ownership, or cost.

Distinguish an available feature, a local example, a proposed feature, and an externally verified result. Keep implementation details out of an ordinary visitor's flow unless they help that person decide. A saved enquiry, an email notification, and a delivered email are separate outcomes. Use precise wording for each outcome the product actually provides.

## Interface content

Labels identify what a person controls. Hints explain a requirement before an error occurs. Errors name the problem and a possible fix. Confirmation distinguishes receipt from completion: “Your enquiry was saved” does not mean an email was delivered.

Make link text understandable in context. Explain an external booking or purchase destination when it changes the visitor's next step. Avoid exposing internal provider names or database concepts unless they help the visitor make a decision.

## Final edit

Read the page in visitor order. Remove duplicate statements, unsupported claims, and sections with no clear job. Check all names, links, contact destinations, prices, and dates against supplied facts. Separate copy review from evidence of real visitor behavior.
