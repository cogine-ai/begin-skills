# Complete example: an article for a service website

## Brief

- **Website:** a fictional independent website consultant's service site.
- **Reader:** a service-business owner preparing a first website with limited writing time.
- **Question:** should a blog be included before launch?
- **Purpose:** help the owner choose useful content and a manageable commitment. It is an educational article, with no sales CTA.
- **Scope:** website content planning; no promised search position, traffic, leads, or universal publication schedule.
- **Material:** original hypothetical examples and practical recommendations. No claimed client experience, interviews, or experiment results.
- **Method:** inspect primary guidance, identify what it supports, then develop an original decision process. Sources were checked on 2026-10-09.

## Article

# Does your first service website need a blog?

A blog is worth adding when you have useful questions to explore and enough time to keep the answers accurate. For a first service website, start by making the service itself easy to understand. Visitors should be able to see whom you help, what the work involves, and how to contact you. An article can then give a particular question the space it deserves.

That makes the decision more concrete. Ask which answer would help a prospective client before a conversation. If you cannot name one yet, finish the essential pages and collect questions as they arise.

## Start with a question someone actually has

Look at the material available to you: notes from conversations, questions in enquiries, or the explanations you find yourself giving. Use only material you can publish appropriately. A photographer might explain how to choose images for a small product collection. A website consultant might compare a simple contact link with an enquiry form.

GOV.UK's [content guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/plan-manage-content/identify-user-needs/) starts with what readers need and recommends examining existing evidence. For a service business, that suggests a useful test: can you explain whose question the piece answers and why the answer matters to them?

You do not need to turn every question into a post. A brief answer about your availability may belong beside the contact details. A fuller explanation of preparing project material may work better as a guide.

## Decide whether you have something useful to add

Before drafting, write the answer you hope the reader will leave with. Then identify what you can contribute: an original example, an explanation of a difficult choice, or practical detail from work you can accurately describe.

Google's [people-first guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks creators to add original value rather than merely rework other sources. This is a useful editorial check. If your article could appear unchanged on any competitor's website, return to the reader's situation and make the explanation more specific.

You can use outside sources to establish facts. Link to the original material, explain the part that matters, and keep your recommendations identifiable. A source describing a feature does not prove that it is the best choice for every business.

## Choose a format you can maintain

A blog is a collection of articles. A single useful guide can stand alone, linked from the relevant service page. Choose the format that makes the answer easiest to find and update.

For example, a preparation checklist may be useful to someone who has already decided to contact you. A comparison article may help someone who is still deciding what they need. Those pieces serve different moments; they do not have to share the same template or ending.

For a first site, a practical approach is to publish one piece you can stand behind, then see which questions remain. Set a review point when a claim, process, or product mentioned in it changes. A schedule only helps if you can sustain the research and editing it requires.

## Write enough to complete the answer

Let the question set the length. Google [states that it has no preferred word count](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content#avoid-creating-search-engine-first-content). A narrow question may need a short explanation; a comparison may need examples and qualifications.

Before publication, read the draft as someone unfamiliar with your work. Check whether the answer is clear, the examples are understandable, and the sources support the facts. Remove passages that repeat a point without helping the reader.

Your first site can begin with clear service pages and one worthwhile guide. Expand the article collection when you have further questions worth answering. The useful commitment is to the quality of each answer and your ability to keep it current.

## Editorial review

- The article answers the launch decision with conditions and alternatives; it does not claim a blog is required or unnecessary for all businesses.
- The source statements are linked in context. The examples and publication approach are independent recommendations.
- No numbers, client outcomes, testimonials, author credentials, or search-performance claims were invented. No source text was quoted.
- The owner must approve the advice and assign accurate authorship before using this as their article. Example roles should be adapted to the site's actual audience.
